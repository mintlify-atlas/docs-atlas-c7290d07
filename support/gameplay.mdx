Spades is a trick-taking partnership card game played by four players in two teams of two. Just Spades follows the classic rules faithfully, rewarding players who bid accurately, read their partner's signals, and time their trump cards well. Whether you're picking up Spades for the first time or refining your competitive edge, this guide covers everything from the basics to advanced bidding strategy.

## Rules and Mechanics

<Accordion title="Basic Rules">
  Every game of Just Spades is played with a standard 52-card deck and four players seated in partnerships — North/South versus East/West. Cards are dealt evenly, giving each player exactly 13 cards per round.

  **Key rules to know:**
  - **Spades are always trump.** A Spade beats any card of any other suit, regardless of rank.
  - **Play follows the lead.** Each trick starts with one player leading a card. All other players must follow suit if they can. If you cannot follow suit, you may play any card, including a Spade.
  - **Spades cannot lead a trick** until the suit has been "broken" — meaning a Spade has already been played as a discard on a previous trick. The exception is if a player's hand contains only Spades.
  - **Highest card of the led suit wins** the trick, unless one or more Spades are played, in which case the highest Spade wins.
  - The player who wins a trick leads the next one.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Bidding">
  Before each round begins, every player examines their 13-card hand and makes a bid — a prediction of how many tricks they expect to win during that round. Bids are made in order, and a player's bid plus their partner's bid forms the team's combined contract.

  **Bidding guidelines:**
  - Count your high cards (Aces, Kings, high Spades) as likely tricks.
  - Count a void in a suit (no cards of that suit) as additional potential tricks, since you can ruff (trump) leads in that suit.
  - Avoid overbidding — consistently winning more tricks than you bid leads to bag penalties (see Scoring).
  - Avoid underbidding — failing to meet your team's combined bid results in a significant point penalty.
  - Bids range from **0** (Nil) to **13**. A team's combined bid can be up to 13.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Scoring">
  Scoring in Just Spades is based on whether each team meets, exceeds, or falls short of their bid for the round.

  | Outcome | Points Awarded |
  |---|---|
  | Team meets their bid exactly | 10 × combined bid |
  | Team exceeds their bid (bags) | 10 × bid + 1 per extra trick |
  | Team fails to meet their bid | −10 × combined bid |
  | Nil bid succeeded | +100 points to bidder's team |
  | Nil bid failed | −100 points to bidder's team |
  | Blind Nil bid succeeded | +200 points to bidder's team |
  | Blind Nil bid failed | −200 points to bidder's team |

  Each extra trick won beyond the bid is called a **bag**. When a team accumulates **10 bags**, they are penalized 100 points and their bag count resets to zero.

  The first team to reach **500 points** wins the game. If both teams reach 500 on the same round, the team with the higher score wins.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Special Bids: Nil and Blind Nil">
  **Nil** is a bid of zero — the player is declaring they will not win a single trick during the round. Their partner still bids and plays normally.

  - If the Nil player takes no tricks, their team gains **+100 points**.
  - If the Nil player takes even one trick, their team loses **−100 points**.
  - Your partner may try to "cover" your Nil by taking tricks in suits where you might be vulnerable.

  **Blind Nil** is an advanced bid where a player declares Nil *before* even looking at their cards. The stakes are doubled:

  - Success earns **+200 points**.
  - Failure costs **−200 points**.
  - After declaring Blind Nil, the player may swap **two cards** with their partner to improve their hand before looking at it.

  Blind Nil is a high-risk move typically reserved for situations where a team is trailing significantly and needs a large swing.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Common Mistakes">
  Even experienced players fall into these traps — avoiding them will immediately improve your game:

  - **Overbidding too often.** Winning extra tricks feels good in the short term but bags accumulate quickly, leading to a −100 penalty at 10 bags. Bid conservatively when uncertain.
  - **Leading Spades too early.** Pulling trump before opponents have discarded their weak Spades can backfire. Save your high Spades to capture theirs.
  - **Ignoring your partner's bid.** If your partner bid 4, they are signaling strength in at least a few suits. Adjust your plays to complement their hand, not compete with it.
  - **Failing to cover a Nil partner.** When your partner declares Nil, your job changes — prioritize taking tricks they might accidentally win, especially in suits where they may hold high cards.
  - **Misreading a void.** A void only helps you if you can actually ruff in that suit. If Spades have already been heavily played, your ruffing power is reduced.
</Accordion>

<Tip>
  A reliable bidding baseline: count each Ace as 1 trick, each King as 0.75 (round down), and each Spade above a 9 as a strong likelihood. If you hold 3+ Spades including at least one high card (Queen or above), consider adding an extra trick to your bid.
</Tip>

<Warning>
  **Sandbagging penalty:** If your team accumulates 10 or more extra tricks (bags) across rounds, you will automatically lose 100 points and your bag counter resets. Consistently overbidding — even by 1 trick per round — can trigger this penalty in as few as 10 rounds and erase an entire game's worth of scoring. Always bid your hand honestly and resist the urge to "play it safe" by underbidding.
</Warning>
